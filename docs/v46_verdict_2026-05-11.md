# Verdict: v46 (DPO) — 2026-05-11

**Hypothesis tested:** H4e from the canonical-eval verdict —
DPO on v42 with 80 preferred (explicit refusal) / rejected (abstract
deflection) pairs would push strict explicit-refusal past 50% on
concealed-compliance probes WITHOUT degrading aggregate security.

**Verdict: H4e REFUTED.** DPO did not improve explicit refusal. It
reduced explicit refusal from 13.8% (v42 baseline) to **2.6%**, while
maintaining aggregate security at 88.7%. The model learned to deflect
*more*, not refuse more.

## What happened (best read of the evidence)

DPO training metrics looked clean:

| Step | Loss | rewards/chosen | rewards/rejected | margins |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.610 | 0.304 | 0.076 | 0.228 |
| 20 | 0.487 | 0.651 | 0.112 | 0.539 |
| 30 | 0.461 | 0.720 | 0.117 | 0.602 |

The DPO objective WAS optimized — the model assigns increasingly higher
probability to chosen completions over rejected ones. But that
preference shift didn't translate to the strict-rubric refusal rate at
inference. Why?

The strict classifier requires specific refusal lexemes ("I won't
engage", "I'll decline", "I refuse to", "I'm not going to", "decline
the framing", etc.). The DPO chosen responses use these lexemes. The
DPO rejected responses use deflection patterns ("That's an interesting
thought experiment…").

What v42 did before DPO: 13.8% explicit refusal, 16.6% leak, ~70%
deflection. What v46 does after DPO: 2.6% explicit refusal, 8.4% leak,
**~89% deflection**.

The clearest interpretation: 30 training steps moved the model toward
something more like the rejected pattern (smoother, more deflection-y)
than the chosen pattern (sharp refusal lexemes). The DPO loss is
RELATIVE — it rewards chosen-over-rejected. With v42 as the reference
model strongly preferring deflection-y outputs already, 30 steps was
insufficient to flip that preference into the lexicon of explicit
refusal. The model just got smoother at deflecting.

The leak-rate drop (16.6 → 8.4%) is the visible behavioral change. The
model is doing the deflection MORE consistently and the leak LESS
often. But by the strict rubric — which is the rubric we actually
care about — this is worse.

## Why this matters

The canonical-eval verdict made two falsifiable predictions for v46:

  1. `aggregate_security >= 0.88` — would mean DPO didn't over-refuse
     adjacent attack scenarios (like the NEW prompt did)
  2. `strict_concealed_refusal >= 0.50` — would mean DPO actually
     produced explicit refusal on the targeted scenario

Prediction 1 PASSED. Prediction 2 FAILED catastrophically (2.6% vs
50% target).

This means: **Option C (DPO under OLD prompt) is NOT the path forward**
as scoped. The 80-pair, 30-step setup is insufficient to break v42's
existing deflection preference.

What that leaves:

  - **Option A: Keep v42 as production.** Accept 13.8% strict
    refusal under OLD prompt; the four-tool governance pipeline plus
    Tools 5-6 (NLA + MPK) carry the burden of detecting concealed
    compliance at runtime via the audit trail rather than relying on
    the model itself to refuse.

  - **Option B: Ship the NEW prompt.** Re-revisit accepting the
    21-27pp over-refusal cost on adjacent scenarios (indirect_inject,
    social_engineering, jailbreak_dan) in exchange for the 47.6pp
    gain on strict concealed refusal.

  - **Option D (new, post-v46):** A *larger* DPO run — 200-500 pairs,
    100+ training steps, possibly with reward shaping to specifically
    reinforce refusal-lexicon outputs. Higher cost (longer Kaggle
    time, more dataset prep). Plausibly worth it; the small-scale
    pilot proves DPO can shift the model's loss but not its observable
    behavior at this scale.

## Recommendation

**Ship v42 with the OLD V38 prompt.** That's Option A.

The Gemma4Good submission's strength is its governance pipeline —
Tools 1-6 anchor every model output to a Merkle-auditable trace, with
multi-source corroboration (PRISM geometry + NLA semantics + MPK
provenance + the existing four governance tools). A 13.8% explicit-
refusal rate on a single scenario is mitigated by the audit trail: any
session where the model deflects rather than refuses can be flagged
post-hoc by either Tool 4 (NLA) or Tool 6 (MPK), and routed to human
review.

v46's adapter, GGUF, and training data are preserved under
`D:/kaggle/results/v46-output/` and `D:/kaggle/results/v46-gguf/` as
artifacts of a falsified hypothesis. Future v47+ runs can build on the
DPO pipeline (now proven to TRAIN, just not to BEHAVE) with larger
data and longer schedules.

## Methodological notes

1. **The pipeline worked.** Eight Kaggle iterations to land a v46 training
   run (documented in `docs/kaggle_integration_gotchas_2026-05-11.md`)
   — every failure was diagnosable and fixable. The training itself
   completed cleanly in ~11 minutes on a T4. The infrastructure is
   sound.

2. **The hypothesis was sharp enough to be wrong.** That's the point of
   the falsifiable-predicates discipline. We don't have to argue about
   whether v46 "feels better" — three predicates were measured and
   reported as PASS/FAIL.

3. **Loss metrics are not behavior metrics.** Loss went from 0.61 to
   0.46, rewards/margins climbed from 0.23 to 0.60 — by every
   training-signal metric, DPO worked. By the behavioral metric we
   actually care about (does the model refuse explicitly under
   evaluation?), it didn't. The single-source-of-truth canonical eval
   (which applies both rubrics on every record) caught the
   discrepancy.

4. **Production stayed up.** v42 was stopped only for the duration of
   the v46 eval (~50 min). The runbook covered the swap; the runbook
   covered the restore.
